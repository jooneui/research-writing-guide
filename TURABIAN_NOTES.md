# Book-based rationale

Source: Kate L. Turabian, *A Manual for Writers of Research Papers, Theses, and Dissertations*, 8th ed. (University of Chicago Press, 2013). Section numbers below refer to this edition. This guide paraphrases selected advice; it does not reproduce the book.

| Guide decision | Relevant sections | Application here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Begin with a question and explain its value | 1.2.5; 2.1–2.3 | The question and **(Why)** keep work tied to a larger research purpose. |
| Build claims from reasons and evidence | 5.2–5.4 | **(Claim)**, **(Reason)**, and **(Evidence)** are separate. The book distinguishes a reason from observable or sourced evidence. |
| Explain why a reason supports a claim | 5.4.4 | If the link is not obvious to readers, state the connecting principle and test its exceptions. |
| Address objections and limits | 5.4.3; 9.1 | Add a limitation or alternative explanation when a finding could be challenged. |
| Repeat key terms consistently | 7.3; 9.3 | Maintain a short term list; reuse the exact technical terms across sections. |
| Interpret figures for the reader | 7.7; 8.3.1 | Introduce a figure's point before it appears, then describe the observation that supports it. |
| Keep figures honest | 8.4 | Preserve fair scales and avoid visual choices that imply a contrast or relation the data do not support. |
| Revise structure before sentences | 9.1–9.4 | Check claim, section role, and paragraph point before polishing wording. |
| Build the paper introduction around a gap | 10.1 | Give context, the unresolved question, its significance, and the claim. |
| Write direct sentences | 11.1 | Use short, concrete subjects and verbs; lead with familiar terms. |
| Cite sources so claims are checkable | 15.1–15.3 | Cite data, methods, borrowed ideas, and figures; give enough detail for a reader to locate them. Use one citation style within a paper. |

## Adaptation for meeting notes

Turabian describes research arguments and papers, not a required weekly meeting note format. The labeled, itemized meeting note structure is this repository's adaptation to the author's preference. In particular, **(Purpose)**, **(Why)**, and **(Deliverable)** describe an action; **(Claim)**, **(Reason)**, and **(Evidence)** evaluate a completed finding. Treating a planned deliverable as evidence would blur a distinction the book emphasizes in section 5.4.2.

**(Why)** is the value of doing the work (the question or decision it serves). **(Reason)** is why a finding's claim should be accepted. These answer different questions and should not be merged.

The section-level sequence is this guide's practical format: introduce the purpose, why, and deliverables; organize the body by deliverable; then state supported findings as claim, reason, and evidence. A deliverable may support multiple findings, and a planned deliverable may have none yet.

## Editorial suggestion

Keep the labels in meeting notes because they make each item's role easy to scan. Use the same reasoning in papers, but express it as coherent prose unless the venue requests a structured format. Requiring a purpose, deliverable, and why for every sentence would add repetition; applying the check to each substantive item, section, and claim preserves the logic while keeping the writing concise.
